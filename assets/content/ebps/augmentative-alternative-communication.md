# Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC)

## What it is

Communication systems and devices that supplement or replace spoken language for individuals with significant communication challenges. AAC includes low-tech (pictures, symbols) and high-tech (speech-generating devices, apps) options.

## Why it matters

Communication is a fundamental human right. When speech is unreliable or absent, AAC provides access to functional communication, reducing frustration and enabling participation in all aspects of life.

## How to implement

1. **Assess communication needs**: What does the student need to communicate? (requests, protests, comments, questions)
2. **Select appropriate AAC system**: Match system to student's motor, visual, and cognitive abilities
3. **Start simple**: Begin with core vocabulary and expand gradually
4. **Model constantly**: Adults must use the AAC system whilst speaking
5. **Respond immediately**: Honour all communication attempts to reinforce use
6. **Integrate across environments**: Use AAC everywhere, not just during therapy
7. **Presume competence**: Never underestimate what the student can communicate given the right tools

### Types of AAC

- **Unaided**: Sign language, gestures, body language
- **Low-tech**: Picture exchange, communication boards, core boards
- **Mid-tech**: Simple voice output devices with buttons
- **High-tech**: Tablets with AAC apps, dedicated speech-generating devices

### Practical examples

- PECS (Picture Exchange Communication System) for requesting
- Core vocabulary board available throughout classroom
- iPad with Proloquo2Go or similar AAC app
- Communication book with personalised vocabulary
- Single-message voice output button for specific routines

## Good to know

- **AAC doesn't prevent speech development**: Research shows AAC often supports speech emergence
- **Core vocabulary is essential**: Focus on versatile words (want, more, help, stop, go) not just nouns
- **Adult modelling is crucial**: Students learn AAC by seeing others use it
- **Presume literacy**: Many non-speaking autistic individuals can read and type
- **Access method matters**: Some students point, some use eye gaze, some scan

## Evidence base

AAC has extensive research support showing improved functional communication, reduced challenging behaviours, increased social participation, and often emergence of speech. Evidence is strongest when AAC is modelled consistently and integrated across settings.

**Related practices**: Functional Communication Training, Visual Supports, Technology-Aided Instruction
