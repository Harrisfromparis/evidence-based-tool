# Naturalistic Intervention

## What it is

Teaching skills within everyday activities and routines rather than in isolated, structured sessions. Naturalistic approaches embed learning in authentic contexts where skills will actually be used.

## Why it matters

Skills learned in natural contexts are more likely to be used in real life. Naturalistic teaching respects student interests and motivations, following the student's lead rather than imposing adult-directed structure.

## How to implement

1. **Follow the student's interests**: Observe what the student is naturally drawn to
2. **Embed learning opportunities**: Use the student's chosen activity as context for teaching
3. **Create need for communication**: Arrange environment so student has reason to communicate (e.g., preferred item slightly out of reach)
4. **Respond naturally**: Provide natural consequences rather than artificial reinforcement
5. **Teach within routines**: Use daily activities (snack, transitions, play) as teaching moments

### Practical examples

- During snack time, teach requesting by waiting for student to ask for preferred food
- Use playground time to teach social skills in authentic peer interactions
- Embed counting practice into naturally occurring activities (setting table, distributing materials)
- Teach turn-taking through naturally motivating games rather than contrived activities
- Use art activities to teach following instructions in engaging context

## Good to know

- **Child-led vs. adult-controlled**: Naturalistic teaching prioritises student interest and choice
- **Requires creativity**: Adults must think flexibly about embedding learning in authentic contexts
- **Works well with DTT**: Combining structured and naturalistic teaching provides balanced approach
- **Builds generalisation**: Skills learned in natural contexts transfer more easily to other situations

## Evidence base

Naturalistic interventions have strong research support, particularly for communication and social skills. Studies show improved generalisation and maintenance of skills compared to isolated training approaches. These methods are highly compatible with neurodiversity-affirming practice.

**Related practices**: Pivotal Response Training, Parent-Implemented Intervention, Functional Communication Training
