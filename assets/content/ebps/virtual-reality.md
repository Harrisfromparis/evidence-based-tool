# Virtual Reality (VR)

## What it is

Using immersive virtual reality technology to create safe, controlled environments where students can practise social skills, explore new places, manage anxiety, and build confidence before facing real-world situations.

## Why it matters

VR provides repeatable, safe practice opportunities for situations that are unpredictable or anxiety-provoking in real life. Students can explore, make mistakes, and build skills without real-world consequences or social pressure.

## How to implement

1. **Identify learning goal**: What skill or situation does the student need to practise or prepare for?
2. **Select appropriate VR content**: Choose scenarios matched to student's goals (social skills, transitions, vocational, life skills)
3. **Introduce VR gradually**: Let student explore comfortable scenarios first
4. **Ensure student control**: Student can pause, exit, or adjust difficulty at any time
5. **Practise with support**: Adult guidance during VR sessions to process experiences
6. **Connect to real life**: Bridge VR practice to actual situations with preparation and follow-up
7. **Monitor comfort**: Watch for signs of discomfort, motion sickness, or sensory overwhelm

### Applications of VR for autism support

- **Social skills practice**: Job interviews, conversations, group interactions
- **Transition preparation**: Virtual tours of new schools, workplaces, buildings
- **Anxiety management**: Gradual exposure to challenging situations in safe environment
- **Vocational training**: Practising workplace tasks and social expectations
- **Life skills**: Navigating public transport, shopping, community settings
- **Classroom behaviour**: Practising expected behaviours in virtual classroom

### Practical examples

- Student virtually explores new secondary school before transition, reducing anxiety
- Practising job interview scenarios 10+ times before real work experience interview
- Virtual supermarket shopping to practise life skills
- Navigating virtual public transport before using real buses
- Practising classroom participation in virtual classroom setting
- Virtual social scenarios to understand perspective-taking and social cues

## Good to know

- **Not for everyone**: Some students find VR uncomfortable, overwhelming, or disorienting
- **Student agency is essential**: Never force VR use; it must be voluntary
- **Supplement, don't replace real practice**: VR prepares for real-world practice, doesn't replace it
- **Sensory considerations**: Monitor for motion sickness, visual strain, and sensory overwhelm
- **Ethical use**: VR should support student goals, not adult convenience

## Evidence base

VR is an emerging evidence-based practice with growing research support. Studies show effectiveness for social skills training, anxiety reduction, transition preparation, and vocational training. Evidence is particularly strong for preparing students for new environments and practising social scenarios. Research indicates VR is safe, acceptable, and effective for many autistic learners when implemented with attention to student comfort and agency.

**Related practices**: Video Modelling, Social Skills Training, Cognitive Behavioural Intervention, Technology-Aided Instruction
