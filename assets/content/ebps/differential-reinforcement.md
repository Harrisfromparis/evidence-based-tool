# Differential Reinforcement

## What it is

Reinforcing desired behaviours whilst withholding reinforcement for unwanted behaviours. This approach strategically uses attention, praise, or access to preferred activities to strengthen specific behaviours.

## Why it matters

Behaviour that is reinforced tends to increase; behaviour that is not reinforced tends to decrease. By being intentional about what we reinforce, we can help students develop more effective ways to get their needs met.

## How to implement

1. **Identify the function**: What is the student gaining from the current behaviour? (attention, escape, access to items, sensory input)
2. **Choose an alternative**: Select a behaviour to reinforce that serves the same function but is more appropriate
3. **Reinforce immediately**: When the alternative behaviour occurs, respond immediately and consistently
4. **Minimise reinforcement for unwanted behaviour**: Reduce attention or access following the behaviour you want to decrease
5. **Monitor and adjust**: Track whether the alternative behaviour increases

### Types of differential reinforcement

- **DRA** (Alternative): Reinforce a different, more appropriate behaviour
- **DRI** (Incompatible): Reinforce behaviour that cannot occur simultaneously with the unwanted behaviour
- **DRO** (Other): Reinforce the absence of unwanted behaviour for a set period

### Practical examples

- Reinforce hand-raising instead of shouting out (whilst ignoring shouting when safe)
- Provide break after 10 minutes of on-task behaviour (DRO)
- Reinforce verbal requests for breaks instead of leaving without permission
- Give access to preferred sensory toy for sitting during group time

## Good to know

- **Never ignore genuine needs**: Do not withhold response to communication about pain, distress, or safety
- **Ensure ability**: The alternative behaviour must be within the student's current capability
- **Avoid harmful extinction**: Some behaviours should not be ignored if they escalate distress
- **Consider self-regulation**: Some behaviours serve important sensory or regulatory functions and should not be eliminated

## Evidence base

Differential reinforcement is one of the most researched behavioural interventions, with strong evidence for effectiveness when implemented ethically and with functional understanding of behaviour.
