# Response Interruption and Redirection

## What it is

Interrupting repetitive behaviours or stereotypy and redirecting the student to more functional activities. This approach is used when repetitive behaviours significantly interfere with learning or daily functioning.

## Why it matters

Some repetitive behaviours serve important regulatory functions and should be respected. However, when repetitive behaviours prevent engagement in learning or daily activities, gentle redirection may be appropriate.

## How to implement

1. **Assess function and impact**: Is the behaviour actually problematic, or just different?
2. **Interrupt gently**: Use least intrusive method to gain attention
3. **Redirect to functional activity**: Guide student to appropriate alternative activity
4. **Reinforce engagement**: Praise participation in redirected activity
5. **Teach alternative regulation**: Provide other ways to meet sensory or regulatory needs

### Important ethical considerations

- **Question the goal**: Does the behaviour actually need to change, or does adult discomfort drive intervention?
- **Respect regulation**: Many repetitive behaviours serve important self-regulation functions
- **Avoid harm**: Never use punishment or physically forceful interruption
- **Student wellbeing first**: If redirection causes distress, reconsider the approach

### Practical examples (when truly necessary)

- Gently redirecting hand-flapping that occurs during instruction by presenting learning materials
- Redirecting repetitive vocalisations during group work to on-task activity
- Interrupting repetitive object spinning to present alternative sensory activity

## Good to know

- **Highly controversial**: This practice raises significant ethical concerns in neurodiversity-affirming approaches
- **Stimming is not inherently problematic**: Repetitive movements often serve important functions
- **Adult perception vs. student need**: Many repetitive behaviours are harmless and should not be targeted
- **Alternatives are better**: Teaching alternative regulation strategies is more respectful than interrupting existing ones
- **Consent and dignity**: Consider student perspective and right to move their own body

## Evidence base

Response interruption has research support for reducing specific repetitive behaviours. However, ethical concerns about respecting autistic self-regulation, bodily autonomy, and neurodiversity have led to significant debate about appropriateness. Current best practice emphasises understanding function before intervention and respecting harmless self-regulatory behaviours.

**Related practices**: Differential Reinforcement, Functional Behaviour Assessment, Antecedent-Based Intervention
