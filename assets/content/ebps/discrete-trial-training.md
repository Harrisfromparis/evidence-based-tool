# Discrete Trial Training

## What it is

Structured teaching method breaking skills into small steps with clear instruction, response, and feedback. DTT uses repeated practice in a controlled format to teach specific skills systematically.

## Why it matters

Some skills are best learned through repeated, structured practice with immediate feedback. DTT can be effective for teaching foundational skills, particularly for students who benefit from clear structure and repetition.

## How to implement

1. **Break skill into steps**: Divide the target skill into the smallest possible teachable units
2. **Give clear instruction**: Provide consistent, simple direction or prompt
3. **Wait for response**: Give the student time to respond
4. **Provide feedback**: Immediately praise correct responses or gently correct incorrect ones
5. **Repeat with variety**: Practise across multiple trials, varying examples and contexts

### Structure of a discrete trial

- **Instruction**: "Touch red"
- **Prompt** (if needed): Point to red card
- **Response**: Student touches red card
- **Consequence**: "Well done!" (or gentle correction)
- **Inter-trial interval**: Brief pause before next trial

### Practical examples

- Teaching colour identification through repeated trials
- Practising letter sounds with immediate feedback
- Learning to respond to name through structured repetition
- Teaching receptive language (identifying objects by name)

## Good to know

- **Intensive format**: DTT is adult-directed and can be demanding for students
- **Motivation matters**: Reinforce with items/activities the student genuinely values
- **Generalisation is critical**: Skills learned in DTT must be practised in natural contexts
- **Not suitable for all skills**: Complex social and communication skills often require more naturalistic approaches
- **Balance with naturalistic teaching**: DTT should be one component of a broader programme, not the only approach

## Evidence base

DTT has substantial research support for teaching specific skills, particularly foundational academic and communication skills. Effectiveness improves when combined with naturalistic teaching methods and when generalisation is explicitly programmed.

**Related practices**: Naturalistic Intervention, Prompting, Reinforcement, Task Analysis
